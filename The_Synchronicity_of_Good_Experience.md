# The synchronicity of good experience

## When brand, product and experience meet the person at the right moment

I have been thinking about synchronicity.

Not as a mysterious force operating inside a banking app or an online shopping basket. I am not suggesting that Carl Jung should replace user research. That would probably be a bit much.

I mean the feeling that something has happened at precisely the right moment. The information we need appears when we need it. A product remembers where we were. The next step makes sense without forcing us to stop and decode it. When something goes wrong, the help offered relates to the problem we are actually having.

The experience seems to understand us.

We often call this intuitive design. Sometimes it is. But intuition does not quite describe the whole thing. A well-designed experience can bring together the promise made by a brand, the capability of a product, the behaviour of an interface and the reality of a customer's situation. When these things align, the result can feel almost synchronistic.

The strange thing is that organisations rarely design them as one thing.

## Customers do not experience departments

Brand teams define promises and personality. Product teams decide what a product will do. UX teams shape interactions. Customer experience teams consider the wider relationship. Accessibility specialists look for barriers. Service teams deal with what happens when the expected journey goes wrong.

There are good reasons for these specialisms. Each requires knowledge and skill. The problem begins when the organisational boundaries become boundaries in the experience.

Customers do not experience a brand strategy, followed by a product strategy, followed by a UX strategy. They experience one organisation.

If a bank promises to be there when customers need it but its app offers no useful help during a difficult payment, the customer does not think the brand team got it right and the interaction team got it wrong. The promise and the experience simply contradict each other.

If a retailer celebrates inclusion in its advertising but its checkout cannot be completed with a keyboard, that is not an accessible design problem sitting quietly at the end of the production process. It is a brand claim that the product cannot support.

If a service describes itself as simple but asks a customer to remember information from three screens ago, simplicity is not being experienced. It is merely being asserted.

This is where synchronicity becomes useful. Not as a new job title, maturity model or hexagon to put on a slide. We have enough of those. It gives us a way to ask whether the separate parts of an organisation come together meaningfully for the person using them.

## What is being synchronised?

Synchronicity has a particular meaning in Jung's work: an apparently meaningful coincidence without an obvious causal relationship. That is not literally what happens in design. Good experiences have causes. They come from research, decisions, technology, testing and a great deal of work that the customer will never see.

Yet the customer's perception can be similar. The right thing happens at the right time, and it feels meaningful.

I think four things need to align.

The first is **promise**. What does the organisation say the experience will be?

The second is **capability**. What does the product or service actually allow the person to do?

The third is **interaction**. Can the person understand, control and complete what they are trying to do?

The fourth is **context**. Does any of this still work in the circumstances in which the person is actually using it?

That context might include disability, fatigue, stress, limited time, a noisy station, an old device, an unreliable connection or a child demanding toast at exactly the wrong moment. People do not become abstract users when they open an app. Life continues around them.

A synchronistic experience occurs when promise, capability, interaction and context align at a meaningful moment.

That sounds straightforward.

It isn't.

## Flow is not the same as movement

UX often describes flow as movement through a sequence. The customer starts here, selects this, confirms that and arrives at the desired outcome. We can map the screens, decisions and system states. We may even remove a few clicks and congratulate ourselves.

But a logically complete flow is not necessarily a coherent human experience.

People pause. They become uncertain. Their circumstances change. They take a different route. They need to check something elsewhere. They make mistakes. Sometimes the system makes the mistake and leaves the customer to sort it out.

If our idea of flow depends on people behaving exactly as expected, we have not designed flow. We have designed compliance with a route.

Perhaps flow is better understood as the continuing alignment between a person and a system. That alignment should survive a pause, an error, a change of input method or a decision to take another route. A good flow does not merely propel somebody forwards. It helps them remain oriented and in control.

This changes what we look for. The question is no longer only, “Can the customer reach the end?” It becomes, “What must remain true throughout the experience?”

Do they know where they are? Do they understand what has happened? Can they change their mind? Is important information available when it becomes relevant? If they leave and return, can they continue? If the system anticipates incorrectly, can they correct it?

The endpoint matters. So does the quality of the journey.

## Synchronicity for whom?

There is an obvious problem with describing an experience as intuitive or seamless. It may be intuitive for the people whose ways of perceiving, thinking and interacting were anticipated by the design.

For everyone else, the seams are often quite visible.

An address lookup may display suggestions at precisely the right moment for a sighted mouse user. If those suggestions cannot be reached with a keyboard or are not announced by a screen reader, the interface has not failed to respond. It has responded selectively.

A payment may provide sixty seconds to confirm a security step. That may feel effortless to one customer and impossible to somebody who needs more time to read, understand or physically complete it.

An error message may be perfectly written and positioned but disappear before someone has processed it. A digital journey may provide complete independence until an exception requires a telephone call. The task can still be completed, but only if the customer surrenders time, privacy or control.

This is why equivalence of experience matters.

An equivalent experience is not necessarily an identical one. Different people may use different interactions, formats or routes. The important question is whether the essential value of the experience survives those differences.

Can people achieve a comparable purpose with comparable information, agency, effort, confidence, dignity and timeliness?

That is a much harder test than asking whether an alternative exists.

“You can call us instead” may provide another route. It is not equivalent if the main route is available at any time, takes five minutes and can be completed privately, while the telephone route operates during office hours and requires the customer to explain why they need help.

Reaching the same administrative endpoint does not prove equivalence of experience.

## Inclusion is not another step in the flow

Inclusive design is sometimes treated as another activity in the production process. Brand makes the promise. Product defines the capability. UX designs the interaction. Accessibility checks it near the end.

That sequencing is part of the problem.

Inclusive design should test the relationship between every part. Who can believe the promise? Who can use the capability? Who can understand and control the interaction? Whose context was considered, and whose was quietly classified as an edge case?

This does not mean every experience can be identical or require exactly the same effort. Human variation makes that impossible. It does mean that significant differences should be understood and intentional rather than discovered by customers after release.

Inclusive design is therefore not simply a way of widening the entrance to an existing flow. It asks whether the flow was based on the right assumptions in the first place.

## Prediction is not understanding

Digital products increasingly try to anticipate what people need. Used well, prediction can reduce effort and make an experience feel remarkably well timed. Used badly, it replaces the customer's intention with the system's guess.

The distinction will become more important as AI takes a greater role in shaping interfaces, content and customer journeys. A system may infer what somebody is trying to do, rearrange the available choices or present what it believes is the next best action.

When the prediction is right, it may feel synchronistic.

When it is wrong, can the person see what has happened? Can they reject the assumption? Can they recover without starting again?

Anticipation should be useful, not compulsory. A product should never make correct prediction a condition of successful use.

There is also a brand question here. If an organisation presents automated prediction as personal understanding, it risks promising more than the product can know. Appearing to understand a customer is not the same as understanding them. Sometimes the most respectful system behaviour is to ask.

## What would we design differently?

Thinking about synchronistic flow would move attention away from isolated touchpoints and towards the relationships between them.

It would ask brand teams whether their promises can be expressed through product behaviour. It would ask product teams whether capability survives real customer contexts. It would ask UX teams whether system changes remain perceptible and controllable through different forms of interaction. It would ask customer experience teams whether alternative routes preserve agency, dignity and time.

It would also change how we judge success.

Task completion tells us whether somebody reached the end. Technical conformance tells us whether defined requirements were met. Satisfaction scores tell us how people answered particular questions. Each is useful. None, by itself, tells us whether promise, product and context remained aligned throughout the experience.

We may need to look for the points at which that alignment breaks.

Where do people lose confidence? Where must they repeat information? Where does one channel forget what another already knows? Where does an alternative route demand more effort or disclosure? Where does the product continue moving while the person is still trying to understand what happened?

Those moments may tell us more than another polished journey map.

## The experience is the organisation

Brand, CX, UX, product design and inclusive design are not interchangeable. They answer different questions and require different expertise. Trying to merge them all into “synchronicity” would stretch the idea until it meant very little.

But the customer encounters their combined effect.

Synchronicity describes the quality of alignment they experience. Equivalence tests whether that alignment is available beyond the organisation's imagined default customer.

That leads me to the argument I keep returning to:

> A synchronistic experience aligns the organisation with the individual. An equivalent experience ensures that this alignment is not reserved for the organisation's imagined default customer.

Good experience design is not simply about making each component work. It is about making the relationships between those components make sense to a person, in context, over time.

When that happens, the experience may feel effortless, intuitive or even coincidental.

It isn't.

It is the organisation keeping its parts in step with the person it claims to serve.
